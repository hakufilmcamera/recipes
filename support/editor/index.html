Getting around

Tap any photo in the library and it opens in the editor where you left it. Nothing you do here is ever baked in: the original is kept, and your edit is a recipe held beside it.

  1. Pick a tab along the side or bottom: Film, Light, Colour, Face, Finish and Print. A dot on a tab means something in it has been changed.
  2. Pick a tool from the row of chips, then drag its slider. The photo updates as you drag, and while you hold a slider everything else steps aside so you see only the picture.
  3. Hold any tool, tab or button to read what it does - the same descriptions as on this page.
  4. Hold the photo to see the original; let go to come back. On the open iPhone Duo, hold Original in the toolbar.
  5. Tap the photo to hide the tools and see only the picture; tap again to bring them back.

Settings › Editor chooses Immersive (the photo fills the screen and the tools float over it on glass) or Framed (the photo above, the tools below), and what the photo sits on.

Undo

Steps back through your edits, one change at a time.

Continuous drags coalesce, so one undo takes back the whole slider move rather than a tick of it. The last forty steps are kept.

Reset

Returns every adjustment to neutral - look, tone, colour, print, retouching, all of it.

When an edit has wandered and starting clean beats unpicking it. The framing stays: aspect, rotation and crop are the picture's shape, not part of the look, and the Crop screen has its own reset for those.

One tap wipes the whole treatment - but it lands on the Undo stack, so Undo brings it straight back.

Film

The Film tab

The look itself: the film strip and your recipes, the strength slider, and the colour path.

Start here - which look, and how strongly, is most of any edit. Hold a look on the strip for its own strength and description.

Expanded, Pragmatic and From RAW decide how the photograph is developed underneath the look; hold those buttons for the full story.

Film simulation

The 3D LUT applied to the frame - the film look itself.

Whichever looks you have imported, in the order you set in Film Looks. Long-press one to pull its strength back - half a strong look is often more usable than all of it.

Applied before the photo is saved, but never destructively - you can change it afterwards without any loss.

Colour path: Expanded, Scene, Pragmatic, From RAW

How the photograph reaches the film look. These film tables were built for a camera that captures far more highlight range than a phone's finished photo carries, so something has to bridge the gap - this is which bridge.

SCENE is the faithful one. It undoes the phone's own rendering using a curve measured from this camera, so the film look receives the light that was actually in front of you and applies at the strength it was designed for.

EXPANDED is punchier. It reconstructs the missing highlights too, but from an estimate rather than a measurement - one that overshoots, which is why film looks can feel heavy-handed at full strength. Every photograph taken before Scene existed was developed this way, which is why it is still here and still the default.

PRAGMATIC is the plainest conversion, with no reconstruction at all.

FROM RAW develops from the DNG, where the highlight range is really there rather than inferred - measured at 2.2 stops more on a window in a dim room. It appears only on photos that carry a RAW file.

Scene against Expanded is the one worth trying on your own photographs. Expanded stretches the tone scale about four and a half stops further than the scene really held - crushing shadows, and near white handing the film table several times the light that was there - so it reads with more contrast and more colour. Whether that is better or merely louder is a matter of taste, and the reason both are offered rather than one being replaced. The cost of Scene is highlights: Expanded invents roughly two stops that were never in the frame, so a white shirt or a bright sky can sit lower on Scene than you are used to. On Pragmatic a white shirt renders around 80% brightness instead of 100%, which looks slightly grey. From RAW takes about a second and a half to develop where the others take milliseconds, and the output is still an ordinary photograph - a film look cannot live inside a RAW file. Its exposure is calibrated to agree with Scene - both land mid-grey exactly where the film tables expect it - so switching between them on a photo with a RAW file compares the two sources honestly. With the film look OFF, compare them with care: the tone curve that shapes a photograph lives inside the film simulation, so with none selected From RAW shows the negative with no curve at all - flat, bright, and with nothing holding the highlights - while Expanded and Scene show the phone's own picture, undone by different amounts. Turn a look on before judging which colour path you prefer.

Light

The Light tab

Tone: Exposure, Contrast, Highlights, Shadows, Shoulder, the point Curve and Selective.

Exposure is applied before the film look, so it moves the scene along the film's own curve - fix brightness here before reaching for anything else. Curve is the S-curve from Lightroom and Photoshop: drag the line into an S for contrast, lift its black end for a faded print, or shape red, green and blue on their own.

Curve and Selective are in the Darkroom.

Exposure

Brightens or darkens the whole frame, in stops. Applied before the film simulation, so it changes where the scene sits on the film's curve rather than just lifting the result.

Contrast

Separation between light and dark. Above 1 deepens shadows and brightens highlights; below 1 flattens the image toward grey.

Highlights

The upper half of the tone curve. Pull it down to recover a sky or a white shirt that has gone flat; push it up to open bright areas. Fades out before it reaches the midtones, so it will not wash the whole frame.

Shadows

The lower half of the tone curve. Up deepens dark areas for a heavier, more graphic look; down opens them and shows what is in them. Note this is the opposite way round from Lightroom, deliberately: here a slider named for a thing gives you more of it. Lifting is scaled so nothing is pushed toward white and made milky.

Shoulder

Fades bright areas into white instead of clipping flat against it, the way film's characteristic curve does. Try it on skies and reflections - digital sensors cut off abruptly where film rolls off.

Curve Darkroom

The point curve, as in Lightroom and Photoshop: drag the line to reshape the tones - pull the lower part down and the upper part up for an S, and the picture gains contrast. Tap anywhere on it to add a point, drag a point off the graph to remove it, move the end points to set the black and white points. RGB, R, G and B curve all three channels or one.

Two things theirs cannot do. The line is a monotone curve, so it never bulges past the points you set - a gentle S never clips. And LUMA mode bends brightness alone and leaves colour where it was, where an RGB curve adds saturation with every bit of contrast. Amount blends the whole curve back toward the picture without it.

Applied after the film look and the colour tools, before vignette and grain: it shapes the finished print, and the grain is never itself curved.

Selective Darkroom

Local editing by tapped points - the tool Snapseed calls Selective, sharper. Tap the photograph to place a point, then adjust brightness, contrast, saturation and structure for just what is under it: the edit clings to the colour at the point, so one placed on a sky holds to the sky rather than drawing a circle on the picture, and fades out at the ring's edge.

Up to eight points. Tap a point to select it, drag it to move it, DELETE to take it out. Points belong to this photograph - a recipe saved from it carries everything else and leaves them behind.

Curve: the RGB curve Darkroom

The curve applied to red, green and blue alike - Lightroom's and Photoshop's point curve.

Contrast with a film's character: an S adds saturation along with the contrast, the way printing harder on colour paper does.

A steep curve moves colour too - skin warmer, skies deeper. LUMA keeps the colour where the film look put it.

Curve: the luma curve Darkroom

The curve applied to brightness alone. Every pixel keeps its colour.

Contrast, a fade or lifted blacks that should not change the colours the film look made - skin above all.

Without the saturation an RGB S brings, the same curve can read flatter; push it a little further.

Colour

The Colour tab

Temp and Tint with the grey card and the R/B grid, Saturation, Vibrance, HSL's eight bands, and the two colour densities.

White balance first - a cast makes every other colour decision wrong. Vibrance lifts what is still muted and spares skin; HSL reaches one colour without touching the rest.

Temp & Tint

The red/blue axis: lower shifts the frame blue, higher shifts it red. Tint is the perpendicular green/magenta axis - the two together reach any cast. Runs before the film sim, so it changes how the sim responds.

GREY CARD is the shortcut: tap it, then tap anything that ought to be neutral - a grey card, a white wall, a sheet of paper - and both numbers follow from it. Hold that button for the rest.

Tint: The green/magenta axis, perpendicular to Temp's red/blue. Between the two sliders any colour cast is reachable; this one earns its keep under fluorescent light, which skews green.

Saturation

Colour intensity. Zero is monochrome. Applied after the film simulation, so it pushes the sim's own palette rather than the camera's.

Vibrance

Saturation aimed at what is still muted. Plain Saturation scales every colour equally, so what is already vivid runs out of room while a pale sky barely moves - Vibrance boosts the muted colour most and leaves near-vivid colour where it is, with skin tones deliberately backed off. Reach for this first; reach for Saturation when you mean everything.

HSL Darkroom

Per-hue colour: pick a band and shift its hue, its saturation or its brightness on its own. This is the one control that can say "just the greens" - which makes it a repair rather than an override, where the Saturation slider above pushes the whole frame and fights the film simulation. Reach for it when one colour is wrong and the rest of the photograph is right: a fluorescent wall, ruddy skin under a warm look, a sky that wants deepening. Neutrals are left alone - a grey has no hue to shift - and only the two bands nearest a colour ever act on it.

Density

Deepens saturated colours - high-chroma areas darken slightly, taking on the weight of a denser dye layer instead of drifting toward neon. This is the control Fujifilm recipes call Color Chrome Effect, and pasting one sets Weak to 0.90 and Strong to 1.0 - measured against a real X100VI, whose chrome runs near this slider's ceiling and whose Weak is about 90% of its Strong.

Blue density

The same deepening, restricted to blue-dominant colour - skies, mostly. Fujifilm recipes call it Color Chrome FX Blue, and pasting one sets Weak to 0.67 and Strong to 0.97 - measured against a real X100VI. The blue detector reaches cyan-blues now; the old one was nearly blind to exactly the skies this exists for.

Face

The Face tab

Retouching, painted where you decide: a smoothing brush, a whitening brush, and a tap-to-heal for blemishes.

Portraits, before the film look is judged - it runs on the source, so the look renders the corrected face rather than painting over it.

Painted, not detected: nothing is retouched where you did not brush.

The whole Face tab is part of the Darkroom.

Smooth Darkroom

Paint over skin to soften its texture - blotch, pores, the week the picture caught you in. Only where you brush. Taps average only with neighbours of similar brightness, so jawlines and hair edges stay sharp on their own, and where Haku finds a face it protects the eyes, brows and lips even inside your stroke. The radius follows the face's size, so one setting does the same work on a headshot and a group. It never reaches fully smooth: skin with no texture at all is the plastic look this avoids.

Whiten Darkroom

Paint over teeth or the whites of eyes to brighten them - desaturating toward neutral and lifting, gated on brightness so shadow is left alone, because whitening a dark mouth interior makes grey fog rather than teeth. One tool for both: they are the same operation on the same kind of surface. Runs before the film simulation, so a warm look warms the result naturally instead of fighting it.

Finish

The Finish tab

Denoise, Sharpness, Clarity, Grain and Size, Vignette, Halation, Split tone, Portrait refocus, Motion blur, Soft blur, Light leak, and the one-tap Effects.

The character drawer. Portrait refocuses by tapping the picture; Effects are whole lighting situations - Flash, Booth, Blue Hour, Backlight - not sliders.

Denoise

Cleans sensor noise from the image before the film look is applied, so the grade acts on the picture rather than on the speckle. Colour noise is removed fully - it carries nothing worth keeping - while half the luminance grain is deliberately left standing, because texture polished to plastic is the tell of cheap noise reduction. Matters most on From RAW, which arrives without Apple's own denoising, and on anything shot at high ISO. Judge it zoomed in: at fit-to-screen the preview has already averaged the noise away.

Sharpness

Fine-detail edge contrast - the crispness of textures, hair, fabric. Below zero it softens fine detail instead, as Fujifilm's own Sharpness does at its negative settings - which is also the antidote to the sharpening the phone's own processing has already applied before Haku sees the frame. NATURAL is that setting in one tap. The counterpart to Clarity: sharpness works at a fine radius on edges, clarity at a wide radius on midtone presence. Both radii scale with the picture, so the editor and the saved file agree. Luminance only, since sharpening colour produces fringes.

Clarity

Local contrast at a wide radius - midtone presence, where Sharpness is fine edges. Push it up and subjects pop; pull it below zero and the frame softens toward a dreamy, diffused look. Weighted to the midtones either way: shadows would only trade noise, highlights would eat the rolloff.

Split tone Darkroom

Two colours pulling at opposite ends of the tone scale - the darkroom's own way of colouring a print, and the look behind most cinema grading. Tone picks the pair.

It is the one colour move nothing else here can make: Temp and Tint shift the whole frame, and HSL only reaches colour that is already present. A split tone works on NEUTRALS - it can put teal into a grey shadow and leave the highlights warm, which is exactly what a plain white wall in shade needs.

Each end keeps to its own half of the tone scale, so midtones and skin stay where they were, and the tint carries no brightness of its own - colouring a photograph should not re-expose it.

Halation Darkroom

The warm glow film gets around bright highlights, from light passing through the emulsion, reflecting off the base and re-exposing from behind. Scatters furthest in red, which is why the halo is orange. Most visible on night lights and windows. Spread sets how far it reaches - tight around highlights, or a dreamier bloom.

Grain

Silver halide texture. Weighted to the midtones the way real emulsion is - shadows are too thin to hold much silver and highlights too dense to show texture, which is why even grain looks digital. Size sets the coarseness: fine suggests a slow film, coarse a fast one pushed in low light.

Blur

Plain softness, like a diffusion filter on the lens - the whole image blurs evenly, and the film simulation and grain act on the softened picture. For streaks that follow movement, use Motion blur instead.

Motion blur Darkroom

Motion blur, as a slow shutter gives a moving subject. The arrow sets the streak's direction - drag it to match the way the subject was moving. Masks: FULL streaks everything, EDGE holds the centre sharp, CENTER the reverse, and PEN lets you paint exactly where the blur lands.

Portrait Darkroom

Refocus after the fact, the way a light-field camera promised: tap the photo to place the focal plane - on your subject, or on the world behind them - and everything else defocuses by how far it sits from that plane. Aperture sets how fast focus falls off. Needs a subject Vision can find; the blur is depth-aware, so the sharp plane never smears into the soft one.

Light leak

A bleed from one corner, as light finds its way past a worn seal and fogs the edge of the roll. Pick the corner it enters from, the colour of the fog - base-fog orange, red, colour-negative magenta, or a cool scan wash - and how far it reaches.

Vignette

Darkens the corners, drawing the eye inward. Lenses do this naturally; here it is a deliberate amount.

Effects Darkroom

One-tap relights: Flash (the camera's own hard light, room falling away - it finds your subject on its own, or tap the photo to aim the burst yourself), Booth (a photobooth's warm monochrome), Blue Hour (the day put out, everything cooled toward the sky) and Backlight (dusk behind the subject). Whole lighting situations, scaled by one strength - your own Light controls act on the relit image.

Print

The Print tab

What happens to the photograph as an OBJECT: the date stamp, Archive wear, Paper textures, Edges - torn, film rebate, tape, deckle, gate - and the Mat.

After the image is right. These read best exported at full size; the mat is the one control here that changes the file's own dimensions.

Date stamp Darkroom

The orange quartz-date stamp in the corner - the photo's own capture date in seven-segment digits, as point-and-shoots printed it. Non-destructive like everything else: toggle it off and re-save and it is gone. The viewfinder never shows it; it belongs to the print, not the scene.

Archive Darkroom

The life of a scanned print: patina, scribble scratches, or dust - pinprick grit with a curving hair or two and the odd lint tangle. Drawn as seeded noise - VAR deals a new pattern of the same kind - and screen-blended, since dust on scanner glass can only brighten.

Paper Darkroom

The surface the print sits on: freckled atelier paper, a magazine's dot screen, a photocopier's streaks, or a flatbed scan's raster. Sits over the grain, under the dust.

Mat Darkroom

A plain border around the picture. Its width is a fraction of the frame's short side, so it is the same thickness on all four edges whatever the shape.

The direction is the real choice. INSET keeps the file the size it always was and scales the photograph down inside the border - nothing is lost, the picture is simply smaller on the same canvas. AROUND grows the canvas instead and leaves the photograph at full size, which makes the finished shape squarer than the picture: a 3:4 frame with an even mat comes out nearer square, exactly as a mounted print does.

Different from Edges > WHITE, which is a print edge - a fine keyline drawn over the picture's own border. This adds a border rather than covering one.

Fisheye Darkroom

A true fisheye projection, not a bulge: lines through the centre stay dead straight and everything else bows away, exactly as a real fisheye lens draws the world. Circle brings the round image floating in black - the 8mm look - with the lens's own falloff and a whisper of colour fringing at the rim. At zero Circle it is a full-frame fisheye, which darkens hard into its corners, as the real ones do.

Edges Darkroom

The border the print was made with: a torn proof rebate, a contact print's black frame, a clean white mat, tape strips fixing it to an album page, a deckled emulsion edge, or a film gate's bitten black surround. Inset sets how deep the border bites; VAR re-deals the organic ones.

Crop and rotate

Crop & rotate

The picture's shape: aspect presets, straightening quarter-turns, and a free crop.

Preset shapes open the crop locked to that ratio, and Flip swaps a locked ratio's orientation. Nothing is destroyed - the full frame is always kept underneath, and Reset inside returns to it.

Saving and sharing

Edits save as you go when you swipe to the next photo (Settings › Library can turn that off, so changes keep only when you press Save). Save keeps the edit with the photo; if the photo is already in Apple Photos, Haku asks whether to update it there or add a new copy.

Save as recipe

Bookmarks this photo's whole treatment as a named recipe you can apply to any other photo.

When a look is worth keeping. A recipe carries the look - film simulation, tone, grain, white balance - never the framing or anything painted on this particular photograph.

A recipe that uses darkroom tools needs the darkroom to save.

Add to Photos

Renders the photo with its current recipe and adds the finished picture to your Photos library.

When this one should live in Photos. The original and its recipe stay in Haku untouched - export again after more editing and you get a second, newer copy there.

Share

Renders at full resolution and hands the finished picture to the share sheet.

Sending to a person or another app. A photo that carries a RAW file sends the DNG alongside the render - two files, because a film simulation cannot live inside a RAW.

The first share renders at full size, which takes a moment at 48 MP; after that it is cached.

Copy Edit

In More: copies this photo's whole treatment, to paste onto others.

Select photos in the library, tap Apply and Paste Edit - see Edit a whole roll. Copying is free; pasting onto many photos at once is part of the Darkroom.
