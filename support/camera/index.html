The basics

Haku puts a film simulation between the sensor and the photograph, the way a real film body does - the colour is built into how the picture is made, not painted on afterwards. Everything else here follows from that one decision.

  1. Choose a look from the row of looks; the viewfinder changes with it, live.
  2. Hold a look to set its strength. Several are strong at full, and somewhere from 40 to 60 is where many settle.
  3. Tap to focus, drag up or down to set exposure, and press the shutter - or the Camera Control on iPhone 16 and later.
  4. Hold any control to learn it. Every card on this page is what that hold shows.

Film looks

Film simulation

The 3D LUT applied to the frame - the film look itself.

Whichever looks you have imported, in the order you set in Film Looks. Long-press one to pull its strength back - half a strong look is often more usable than all of it.

Applied before the photo is saved, but never destructively - you can change it afterwards without any loss.

Grain

Silver halide texture, applied after the film simulation the way it sits in an emulsion.

Amount is how much texture, size is how coarse the clumps are - fine suggests a slow, fine-grained stock, coarse a fast one pushed in low light. The viewfinder shows it live, so set it by eye.

Weighted to the midtones, because shadows are too thin to hold much silver and highlights too dense to show texture - even grain across the frame is what makes digital grain look fake. Set here it applies to every shot until changed; the editor adjusts a single photo afterwards.

Exposure

Shutter speed

How long the sensor is exposed to light, in fractions of a second.

Fast (1/500 and up) freezes motion. Slow (1/30 and below) lets it blur and gathers more light in the dark. Set only this dial and leave ISO on A for shutter priority: your speed holds and the ISO keeps metering.

Below about 1/60 handheld, your own movement starts to blur the whole frame. Set it to A and the camera picks.

ISO

How strongly the sensor's signal is amplified.

Keep it low (125-400) in good light. Raise it indoors or at night so you can keep a usable shutter speed. Set only this dial and leave shutter on A for ISO priority: your ISO holds and the shutter keeps metering.

Higher ISO means more noise. On this sensor anything past about 3200 gets visibly grainy.

Exposure compensation

Nudges the camera's automatic exposure brighter or darker, in stops.

Snow and bright sand fool the meter into underexposing - dial in +1. Stage lighting and dark backgrounds fool it the other way.

Only does something when ISO or shutter is on A. In full manual you are already deciding exposure yourself.

Metering

How the camera reads the scene to set exposure. MULTI weighs nine zones and protects the subject, CENTRE leaves it to the phone, SPOT reads where you tapped, and HI-LIGHT protects the brightest part of the picture.

MULTI for most things - it is the default and it mostly agrees with the phone. SPOT when the subject is much brighter or darker than its surroundings and you have tapped to meter off it; the point stays until you move it, unlike tap-to-focus. CENTRE to take Haku's meter out of it entirely. HI-LIGHT for stage light, a window, a white shirt in sun - anywhere blown highlights would ruin the frame and dark shadows would not.

iOS publishes no metering-pattern control, only a metering point, so CENTRE and SPOT are that point placed differently. MULTI and HI-LIGHT are metered by Haku from the live frame. MULTI weighs nine zones centre-first and steps in only when the middle disagrees with its surround by more than a third of a stop - a backlit face opened up, a spotlit one held back - so a balanced scene gets nothing at all and your photographs are not quietly re-exposed by a mode you did not choose. HI-LIGHT holds the exposure down by up to two and a half stops to keep the top end off the ceiling, letting go slowly so the picture does not pump as bright things pass through.

AE lock

Holds the exposure the meter has settled on, so recomposing does not change it.

Point at the part of the scene you want exposed correctly, lock, then recompose and shoot. Dragging up or down on the viewfinder to set exposure locks it for you, since a compensation is only meaningful against a reading that stays put.

Lit, the key reads RESET: pressing it puts exposure back to the meter's own reading and releases the lock, undoing any compensation in one press. The lock holds the meter, not you - you can still dial exposure up or down while it is on.

Focus

Focus

Where the lens is focused. A tracks the subject automatically; manual pins it to a fixed distance.

Manual is useful for shooting through glass or fences, which confuse autofocus, and for pre-focusing on a spot you know someone will walk into.

Depth of field on a phone is deep, so manual focus is less critical than on a real camera - but peaking shows you exactly what is sharp.

AFL - focus lock

Holds focus where it is instead of continuously refocusing.

Focus on your subject, lock, then recompose without the camera hunting. Also handy when shooting several frames at the same distance.

If your subject moves closer or further away, the shot goes soft - unlock to let it track again.

Macro

Lets the camera focus much closer than it otherwise can - a few centimetres rather than about ten.

Small things: texture, flowers, detail on an object. Turn it on when the camera refuses to focus because you have moved too close.

It works by switching to the ultra-wide lens and cropping back to your framing, so the picture stays where it was but the resolution drops. That is the same trade the stock camera makes, and at this distance the main lens cannot focus at all.

PEAK - focus peaking

Outlines the parts of the frame that are actually in focus.

Checking that focus landed on the eye rather than the ear, macro work, or any scene where the difference between sharp and nearly-sharp decides the shot. It measures where an edge completes its transition - a focused edge does it within a pixel, a defocused one spreads it - so a contrasty edge that is slightly soft stays dark rather than lighting up.

Viewfinder only - it is never baked into a photograph. Busy textures that ARE in focus all light up, whether or not they are your subject, and in very low light sensor noise can sparkle faintly - noise is fine detail too, just not the kind you are focusing.

Colour

White balance

What the camera treats as neutral white, measured in kelvin.

Lower values (3000K) cool the image down under warm indoor bulbs. Higher (7000K) warms it up in shade or overcast light.

Applied before the film simulation, exactly as on a film camera, so it changes how the film sim responds rather than just tinting the result.

White balance presets

What the camera treats as neutral white, as named lighting rather than a number.

AUTO lets the camera judge. DAY 5500K for sunlight, CLOUD 6500K and SHADE 7500K warm up dull light, TUNG 3200K cools warm indoor bulbs, FLUO 4200K handles strip lighting. Picking any of them switches to manual and reveals kelvin and tint sliders for fine work.

Applied before the film simulation, exactly as on a film camera, so it changes how the sim responds rather than just tinting the result.

Grey card

Point at something that ought to be neutral and the light follows from it.

Tap it, then tap a grey card, a white wall, a sheet of paper, a road - anything you know has no colour of its own. Zoom in first if the neutral thing is small; the tap follows the picture. The temperature and tint that make that spot grey become the photograph's white balance.

It measures the LIGHT, not the look. The finished photograph may still not be neutral at that spot, because a film simulation shifts colour on purpose - Classic Neg. is not trying to leave your wall grey. Nothing wrong has happened; white balance runs before the film sim, exactly as on a real camera. It reads the scene rather than the render, so an existing white balance does not compound - the answer is always absolute.

Framing

Lens

The focal lengths this phone actually has, with the 35mm equivalent under whichever one is selected. Tap the main lens again, when you are already on it, to cycle it between its own focal length and 35mm.

35mm is the classic reportage length and what a certain fixed-lens camera is built around - a phone's main lens is wider than most people who reach for a prime actually want. It sticks until you change it, so the camera can simply be a 35mm.

35mm is the main lens cropped rather than a lens of its own, so it costs resolution - about 22MP of the sensor's 48 on this phone, still well clear of 12, which is why RES keeps meaning something there where at 2x it does not. Nothing has to change over, so it is reachable in the middle of a recording. Stops that need a different lens are dimmed while recording, because starting one would put a hole in the file.

Focal length crops

Centre crops that match what a longer lens would have seen, from this photo's own focal length in its EXIF.

Shot at 24mm and wish it had been a 35? The chip is that exact crop - the camera's 35mm prime, after the fact. It sets the box; drag to reframe, and the ratio lock still shapes it, so 35mm at 1:1 keeps the 35's horizontal field in a square.

A crop spends pixels: 24mm to 85mm keeps well under a tenth of the frame. The library's MP tag tells the truth about what is left.

Frame shape

4:3 is the whole sensor. 3:2, 16:9, 1:1 and 6:7 are crops of it.

4:3 to keep everything. 3:2 is the classic 35mm shape; 6:7 is the medium-format one, taller than it is wide. Pick the shape while you are framing rather than cropping later - composing to the edges is the point.

None that costs you anything. The crop is applied when the photo is rendered and the full sensor frame is always kept, so changing your mind later in the editor gives back every pixel.

Composition grid

Rule-of-thirds lines over the frame.

Placing a horizon or a subject on a third rather than dead centre usually makes a photograph sit better. Also the quickest way to keep verticals straight.

Electronic level

Artificial horizon showing how far the camera is tilted.

Landscapes, architecture, anything with a horizon or strong verticals - a degree or two off is very visible once printed.

Reads roll only, not forward tilt. It turns amber within a degree of level.

Front / back

Switches between the rear cameras and the selfie camera.

The obvious reason. The film simulation, every manual control and the whole editor work identically on either.

The front camera is a single fixed lens, so the zoom stops and macro go away while it is selected, and it offers less resolution than the rear 48MP sensor.

Capture

Photo quality

How much processing Apple applies to the frame before Haku ever sees it.

Only does anything at 12MP, in dim light - that is where FINE's multi-frame denoising runs. Leave it on BAL by day and reach for FINE indoors or at night.

Measured on this phone: in good light the two are the same shot at the same speed. At ISO 1200 and 12MP, FINE takes 1.2s against BAL's 0.5s and returns a visibly cleaner file. At 48MP they are identical in every light - the denoising runs on the binned 12MP path, so there is nothing for it to do at full resolution, which is why this greys out there.

Resolution

12MP or 48MP. The sensor will not give both full resolution and a fast viewfinder.

12MP for everyday shooting: a 60fps viewfinder and a shutter around 0.6 seconds. 48MP when detail matters more than pace - landscapes, anything you will crop into or print large.

Two things cost it, and the body says so under the shutter when they apply. Zoom: past 1x the camera crops the sensor, so resolution falls with the square of the zoom and floors at 12MP. Flash: a 48MP frame is built by stacking several exposures and a single burst of light cannot be stacked, so any flash shot comes back 12MP. RAW does not: ProRAW goes to full resolution.

Flash

Off, auto, or always on. Tap to cycle.

Off is right almost always - the sensor is better in low light than the flash is, and a direct flash flattens a face and kills the colour a film simulation is trying to make. Worth it for fill against a bright window, or to freeze something moving in the dark.

Any flash shot comes back 12MP, whatever the resolution button says, and the line under the shutter says so while flash is armed. This is the phone, not Haku: in low light the sensor reads out binned - four pixels combined into one for the light they gather together - and that readout is 12MP. The stock camera does exactly the same thing, which is worth knowing before assuming it is a bug here.

Self-timer

Off, 3 seconds, or 10. Tap to cycle.

Getting into the picture, or getting your hands off the phone before a long exposure - at anything under about 1/30 the press itself is what blurs the frame.

Deliberately not remembered between launches, unlike most of the body. A timer you had forgotten about costs you the shot you were actually trying to take.

Photo / video

Which one the shutter takes. In video mode the shutter starts and stops a recording, RES and the FPS key take over the video settings, and flash becomes the torch.

Switch when you mean to film. Holding the shutter records without switching, which is the one for something that will not wait.

Video mode owns the sensor while it is on - it selects a format that can hold the frame rate you asked for, and that costs RAW and 48MP stills for as long as you stay in it. Leaving hands the photo pipeline back whole. The recording itself is kept clean and the film simulation is a recipe over it, exactly as a photograph is: playback grades live, and the look, the trim and the frame shape are all still yours afterwards.

Video resolution

4K or HD, both 16:9. Video mode takes the sensor for itself, which is what makes 4K possible at all.

4K for anything you might crop or keep; HD when you want smaller files and cooler running - a long 4K clip is the one thing here that will warm the phone.

Not every resolution exists at every frame rate on this sensor. The app picks the closest format it can actually stream, and the readout reports what was really delivered rather than what was asked - so if you set 4K and see HD, the format for that pacing did not exist.

Frame rate

24, 30, 60 or 120 frames a second. 24 is the cinema rate; 30 is what phones default to; 60 and 120 are for motion and for slowing it down afterwards.

24 for anything meant to look filmed. 30 for ordinary recording. 60 for movement you want smooth, 120 when you intend to slow it down in editing.

High rates need particular sensor formats, and some of them cannot deliver the pixel layout this app's film simulation reads. When that happens the camera keeps the format it had and the readout tells you the rate you actually got. Video mode holds the sensor while it is on; leaving it hands 48MP and RAW back to photos.

Long and multiple exposures

Long exposure Darkroom

Exposures far past the one-second shutter limit: the camera integrates seconds of frames into one picture.

SILK averages everything - water turns to glass, crowds thin away, noise drops with every second. TRAIL keeps each pixel's brightest moment - car lights write lines, stars write arcs. Press the shutter to start, watch the picture build in the finder, press again to stop early and keep it. Brace the phone or use a tripod, and the self-timer to start without touching it.

The stack comes from the sensor's stream at 12MP, with no RAW. Handheld shake blurs the whole frame, not just what moves - this is the one control in the app that genuinely wants a tripod.

Multiple exposure Darkroom

Two to nine deliberate photographs on one frame, the way a film camera does it when you wind back and shoot again.

Set how many, then take them one press at a time - what is already down appears faintly over the finder so the next one can be placed against it. Tap a STACK number again mid-set to throw the set away.

JOIN decides how they meet. AVG keeps the exposure correct with no compensation and is where to start. ADD is what film literally does, light adding to light, so it blows out unless each frame is darker than usual - which is the look. LIGHT keeps each pixel's brightest moment, the classic way to put a face into a dark sky; DARK keeps the darkest.

CLONE is the different one: it finds the SUBJECT of each new frame and lays just them onto the picture, solid. That is how you stand in your own photograph twice, or nine times - every other setting mixes the frames where they overlap, which is what makes a person transparent. Rest the phone somewhere it will not move, shoot, move, shoot again.

CLONE wants a still camera and a clear subject - a person, an animal, an object apart from its background. Each exposure after the first contributes ONLY what reads as a subject in it: a second animal wandering through may or may not make the cut frame by frame, and anything it misses survives only if it was in the first frame, which is kept whole. Where no subject is found at all it falls back to keeping whatever changed, which works best against a plain scene. The whole set is shot with the phone the same way up - a turned frame is refused rather than stretched - though changing LENS mid-set is fine. RAW is skipped while a set runs, since the negative of one exposure of a set develops to a photograph nobody took.

The mat

Mat: around

The canvas grows by the border on every side and the photograph keeps its own size. Nothing about the picture changes - it simply gains a margin.

The usual one, and the default. It is what mounting a print means: the picture is what it was, and there is board around it.

The finished shape changes, because the border adds the same number of pixels to a short side as to a long one. A 3:4 frame with an even mat comes out nearer square - which is exactly what a mounted print looks like, but it does mean the file is no longer the aspect you shot. The file is also larger by the border.

Mat: inset

The canvas keeps the dimensions it already had and the photograph is scaled down inside the border instead.

When the output size matters more than the picture's size - a fixed frame to post into, or a set of images that must all come out the same shape.

The picture ends up smaller in the file, so there is less of it to print or crop into later. Nothing is cut off: it is scaled to fit and keeps its own proportions, which is why the border can end up a little wider on two sides than the other two.

Instruments

Histogram

Distribution of brightness across the frame - shadows on the left, highlights on the right.

Watch for a trace piling up against either edge. The white line is overall brightness; the red, green and blue traces are the channels, and a saturated subject blows one of those long before the white one reaches the edge. Percentages appear in the corners when pixels are actually clipped.

Striped areas in the preview are blown highlights. Some clipping is fine - specular reflections are meant to be white.

Waveform

Brightness plotted across the frame - left to right matches the picture, bottom to top is dark to light.

When you need to know WHERE the exposure is, not just whether it is right. A blown sky on one side and a face in shadow on the other are one lump on the histogram and two separate shapes here. The three faint lines are the darkest tone that still holds texture, 18% grey where a face belongs, and the brightest tone that still holds texture.

It reads the whole frame every eighth of a second, so it costs a little more than the histogram. Anything above the top line or below the bottom one has no texture left in the print.

ZEBRA - blown highlights

Stripes over anything bright enough to have lost detail - measured on the capture itself, before the film look, so a look with a gentle shoulder cannot hide a blown sky.

Watching skies, white clothing and skin under hard light. If the stripes are on your subject, pull exposure down.

Viewfinder only. Some clipping is correct - specular reflections and light sources are meant to be white, so stripes are information rather than an error.

Setups and shortcuts

Q - quick menu

White balance presets and the three viewfinder instruments, in one strip.

Reach for it when the light changes - the presets are faster than dialling kelvin - or when you want to check focus and exposure before committing to a shot.

The button lights up while any instrument is on, so you can tell at a glance that something is being drawn over the frame even with the menu closed.

C1 and C2 - custom setups

Two slots that remember how the camera is set up, so a way of shooting is one press away.

Set the camera how you like it, then press an empty slot to save it. Pressing a filled slot puts everything back, and pressing it again while lit returns to how things were before. Hold either one to replace or clear it.

Stores the film look and every adjustment, plus resolution, RAW, quality and frame shape. It leaves out focus and exposure locks, manual ISO and shutter, the timer, flash and zoom - those belong to one particular scene, and restoring them silently would cost you a shot rather than save you time.

Lock

Freezes every control except the shutter, for carrying the camera in hand between shots.

Walking with the app open, fingers wrapped around the screen. A real camera's grip has no controls; a phone's screen is all controls, and a grazed key can re-arm the shutter, save a preset or move the exposure without you noticing. Locked, only the shutter fires - the shot is always available.

Hold the amber HOLD TO UNLOCK pill - it sits in the body's own border, never over the picture - to release. On Field, the speaker grille in the top corner locks; on Classic it is Q > GUIDES > LOCK. The lock does not survive closing the app - a camera that launches locked tomorrow would read as broken.

Reset

Clears every capture adjustment back to the look alone.

Keeps the film simulation and your frame shape; exposure shift, white balance, grain and the rest return to neutral. It only changes what the next shot carries - photographs already taken keep the recipes they were made with.

There is no undo on the camera side - though a saved recipe or a C1/C2 preset brings a setup back in one tap.
